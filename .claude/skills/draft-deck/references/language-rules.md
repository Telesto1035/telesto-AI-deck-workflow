# Language rules

Principles for titles, claims, provenance, and craft. Stable; changed only by the skill owner. Rules learned from shipped decks live in `house-register.md` (capped, governed); client wording and formatting in `clients/<client>.md`.

House style for titles, takeaways, and slide text. Applies on both papers (thinking + craft always apply; only branding changes by paper). Examples are drawn from shipped decks.

The standard is general professional consulting writing: an MBB or Big Four deliverable, a memo to an F500 executive. Every rule below is meant to be one a reader outside the firm could apply the same way. None of it is scored; deck-qc retired the register dimension on 2026-09-15 because voice varies between competent consultants and a taste verdict cannot be calibrated or shared. These rules produce items that quote the line they rest on, or they produce nothing.

## How this file is read at each stage

These rules were written for drafting: they say how to write a page that does not exist yet. A deck is drafted once and revised five or more times, so most readings of this file happen against a page that already exists, usually one a consultant has edited by hand.

**At revision the drafted line is the baseline.** An item must name what is wrong with the line against the evidence, the source or the instrument, not name what a fresh draft would have done differently. "Another draft would have phrased this another way" is not a finding, and neither is a line that merely fails a rule below while saying something true, sourced and clear.

Lines marked *(drafting craft)* shape a first draft and then step back: they produce no revision items at all. Everything else produces items, and the standing rule holds over all of it: a language rule is a FIX only when it names a defect, never when it names a word.

The distinction is the one the machine-phrasing rule already states below: **the test, not the list, is the rule.**

## Action titles

The title IS the takeaway. A reader skimming only titles gets the full argument.

- Full sentence, verb-led, specific, ~8–20 words. *(drafting craft)* A finished title that runs longer and lands is not a defect; the band describes a length that reads well, not a bound to enforce.
- Good: "Buyers select based on peer referrals, higher-ed experience, and personalized proposals, rather than cost."
- Good: "When forced to prioritize, leaders rank financial sustainability and AI as top challenges, while deprioritizing public trust and student preparedness."
- Bad: "Selection factors" (label, not takeaway). Bad: "[Client] dominates the market" (over-claim).
- No label prefix on a title. "Where to play:", "Who to target |" and "What to say |" were removed from every title on a shipped findings deck; the storyline position is carried by the eyebrow or the section divider, and the title is the assertion alone. The older series-prefix pattern (`Mainstream | …`) is retired.
- Two-part titles (title + subtitle continuation, "…despite top challenges' lack of resource and leadership attention") are allowed sparingly for tension between two facts. *(drafting craft)* “Sparingly” is a budget across a draft, and it cannot be judged from a page in front of you.

## Sizing claims to evidence

- **No superlatives, no vague intensifiers.** Banned unless quoting: massive, dramatic, clear winner, game-changing, unprecedented, extremely (outside scale labels). If it matters, give the number. The claim is the rule and the list is its evidence: an unquantified superlative is a revision item, a word match on its own is not. "Significant" was removed from this list on 2026-09-16 because it is a statistical term, and a survey deck reporting significance testing is using it correctly.
- **No "every" or "never" claims from single-survey data.** "Outperform every paid channel" becomes "outperform paid outreach channels". Totalizing words survive only when the data is literally total.
- **Quantify deltas**: "26pts difference", "2X the rate", "[X]% vs. [Y]%" — never "far more" alone. ("far more than the C-suite (26pts difference)" is fine: adjective + number.)
- **Verbs scale with evidence.** One data point: "indicates", "points to", "suggests". Converging data points: "shows", "finds". Reserve "confirms"/"proves" for near-tautologies, and prefer not to use them.
- **Subgroup findings stay subgroup findings.** "Tech leaders elevate cybersecurity sharply" — do not promote to "leaders elevate cybersecurity".
- **Small n:** label cuts "directional only"; state exclusions ("Chartis and CCS have low end counts (n<18); … excluded (n<5)"). State margin of error in methodology.
- **Tension is stated, not hidden**: "even though a larger share say partners' AI tools justify their fees" — keep the countervailing number next to the headline number.
- **No legal or regulatory judgments** the dataset cannot support. Describe what was observed; do not conclude compliance or violation.

## Provenance — every number has a home

- Chart subtitle convention: `% of respondents selecting attribute · n=200` (metric · denominator).
- Question sourcing in footnotes: `Q12: Which of the following cost reduction strategies…`. Cross-survey borrowings named: "From Competitive Intelligence survey: …".
- Quotes verbatim, in quotation marks, with attribution: `"…" (Board of trustees, R1)`.
- Missing number → `[PLACEHOLDER: source needed]`. Never estimate and present as fact. Never reuse a number from a different base without re-stating the base.
- **Labels are provenance too.** An axis, banner or category label that traces to the instrument may be shortened for the reader, but the source wording survives somewhere on the page — footnote with the question number, or the axis itself. Shortening it in every place at once is a decision for the consultant, not a craft edit, and a short form that merges two categories the instrument keeps apart changes what the number means (Revise, class `label`).

## Finding vs. implication

Two registers, kept separate:
- **Finding**: declarative, data-anchored. "AI is changing what buyers will pay for, posing material risk to 2027 revenue. [X]% agree [statement]…"
- **Implication**: imperative, action-anchored, labeled. "Implication: Position [the client's] modernization work as the safe foundation for the next deployment…"

A finding never smuggles in a recommendation; an implication never introduces new data.

## Craft

- Active verbs. "Leaders rank financial sustainability highest", not "Financial sustainability was ranked highest by leaders". *(drafting craft)* Voice-review is explicit that passive in itself is not a fault; on an existing page, recasting a working sentence buys nothing.
- Verbs stay verbs: "decide", not "make a decision"; "prioritize", not "conduct prioritization". *(drafting craft)*
- Short sentences. *(drafting craft)* The old form of this rule split any sentence carrying more than one "that/which/as well as", which is a counter, not a judgment, and it fired on correct sentences. Length is a drafting habit; at revision a long sentence is an item only where it is genuinely hard to read.
- No filler, no empty transitions, no consulting hollow-speak (force multiplier, boil the ocean, synergies).
- No AI-sounding constructions: no triplet-parallel slogans, no "It's not X. It's Y." framing, no "already has / already operates" filler. Save catchphrase constructions for when a catchphrase is asked for, not decks.
- Phrases that read as machine-written are out on every paper. These, flagged by the consultant on the Vanguard Standard scaffold (2026-09-13), are examples and not the whole list: "the chain cannot guess", "what a client keeps using", "it already has", "after the deck has been read", and any title that personifies a tool or workflow as knowing, guessing or deciding. The test, not the list, is the rule: would a consultant write this sentence in an email to the CEO? If not, rewrite it as a plain statement of what the reader gets or does. The recurring failure is an abstraction handed a verb a person should own, as in "the job buyers keep for a firm", "work that shapes the implementation", "judgment Huron already sells" or "the deck earns the right to the strategy conversation".
- Process notes are not titles. Instructions the consultant gives the builder ("the section ends there", "held until tested", "write this page last") describe the build and never appear on a slide. If a note has to reach the reader, it becomes a status marker (IN DEVELOPMENT) or a footnote, not a title clause.
- No em dashes (—) in original prose: use commas, periods, or restructure. Verbatim quoted material (question text, quotes) keeps its own punctuation. On a page that already exists this is a SUGGEST and never applied for the consultant: it is punctuation across a whole deck, not a defect on one line.
- Parallel structure within any list or numbered series: same grammatical shape, same level of specificity.

## Claims precedent (client paper)

Before drafting on client paper, skim the attached shipped deck for how far claims go (does this client accept "material risk to 2027 revenue"? Do they hedge everything?). Match that level. Any escalation beyond precedent gets flagged to the user in the draft, marked for account-partner sign-off. HOW to quantify is craft; WHETHER to introduce a new claim type is precedent.
